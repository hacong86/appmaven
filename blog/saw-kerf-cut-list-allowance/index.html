Saw Kerf Explained: Allow for the Width of Every Cut

See why three 12-inch parts can need 36¼ inches of stock, distinguish kerf from blade plate thickness, and count trims separately.

AppMaven TeamOctober 18, 2026Woodworking · Practical guide

In this guide

  1. Kerf and plate thickness
  2. The 36¼-inch example
  3. Count the cuts your layout needs
  4. Separate kerf from other allowances

Three 12-inch parts add up to 36 inches of finished material. That does not automatically mean a 36-inch board will provide them. A cut removes material between the pieces, and that width needs a place in the layout.

Kerf and plate thickness

Kerf is the width of the cut. It is a different specification from the thickness of the blade’s metal body, often listed as the plate. For example, Freud lists its P410T blade with a 0.091-inch kerf and a 0.071-inch plate. [1] This is an example of two distinct specifications, not a blade recommendation.

For material planning, use the cutting width appropriate to the blade and setup you will use. A convenient fraction such as ⅛ inch can illustrate the arithmetic, but it is not a universal kerf setting.

The 36¼-inch example

Here is a deliberately simplified one-dimensional layout: three finished parts are each 12 inches long, there are exactly two separating cuts, and both outer ends are already acceptable. There is no extra end trimming and no additional cut separating a leftover piece. Assume a kerf of ⅛ inch for this example.

Required length = 3 × 12 + 2 × ⅛ = 36¼ inches

The two kerfs consume ¼ inch in total. A stock length of exactly 36 inches leaves no material for those two cuts. The calculation describes a length budget, not a sequence of machine operations.

Same parts, different explicitly assumed allowances
AssumptionStock length needed
36 in of parts + two ⅛ in kerfs36¼ in
36 in of parts + three ⅛ in kerfs36⅜ in
36 in of parts + three ⅛ in kerfs + ½ in total additional trim/waste36⅞ in

Count the cuts your layout needs

“Number of parts minus one” only describes the separating cuts in the first example. If the actual layout also needs a cut between a finished part and a leftover, that is another kerf. If an end must be trimmed, include the material consumed by that operation too.

As a planning worksheet, list each finished part, each cut allowance and each other waste allowance. Add the lengths only after those entries are explicit. For four 10-inch parts with exactly three 0.1-inch separating kerfs and no other waste, the total is 40 + 0.3 = 40.3 inches. Change the cut count or kerf and the answer changes.

Separate kerf from other allowances

Kerf accounts for material removed by cuts. End waste, edge trimming and material reserved for later fitting are separate planning entries. When an allowance is entered as a total amount consumed, make clear whether its cut width is already included; do not count the same loss twice.

For sheet goods, total part area alone cannot establish that the parts fit. A layout also has to place the parts, spaces for cuts and any edge trim within the usable sheet dimensions. Record those inputs before trusting a material count.

Pair the layout with a cut list that identifies the finished parts. This article explains material accounting; use the equipment manufacturer’s instructions for actual cutting operations.
