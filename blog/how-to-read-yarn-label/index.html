How to Read a Yarn Label: A Practical Guide

Learn how to read a yarn label, compare yarn weight and yardage, check dye lots and care instructions, and estimate skeins for your next project.

AppMaven StudioNovember 9, 2026Knitting & crochet

A yarn label is a small guide to a surprisingly large set of decisions. It can tell you what a yarn is made of, approximately how much is in the package, how the maker recommends caring for it, and where to begin when choosing a hook or needles. Knowing how to read a yarn label helps you compare options before you start a knitting or crochet project—and gives you useful details to keep after the paper band comes off.

Start with your pattern. Check its recommended yarn, total length and gauge, then use the label to assess the yarn in front of you. The label is a starting point: the fabric you make and the pattern's requirements still matter.

Yarn label fields at a glance

Label detail What it tells you What to check
Fiber content The materials in the yarn, often with percentages Whether the feel, care and properties suit the project
Yarn weight category A general thickness group, often numbered 0–7 Compare with the pattern, then make a gauge swatch
Net weight The mass of the yarn in the package, often in grams or ounces Use it with the length, not as a substitute for yardage
Length or yardage How much yarn is in one skein, ball or hank Compare with the total length your pattern requires
Color and dye lot The shade and, where provided, the batch identifier Check lot numbers when buying multiple packages
Suggested tools and gauge The maker's starting point for hook or needle size and stitches over a given distance Follow the pattern's gauge and swatch with your chosen yarn
Care instructions The maker's recommended washing, drying and other care Keep these instructions for the finished item

Not every label uses the same layout or includes every field. Check the maker's product information if an important detail is missing.

Yarn weight versus package weight

The phrase yarn weight usually describes a yarn's thickness category, not how heavy a single ball is. The Craft Yarn Council's system runs from category 0, lace, through category 7, jumbo. Categories help you narrow a search, but they are broad guides: yarns in the same category can still produce different fabrics or gauges.

The net weight printed in grams or ounces describes the mass of the yarn in that package. Two 100-gram skeins need not contain the same length: fiber, construction and thickness all affect how much yarn fits into that mass. A label might therefore show both a category number and a measurement such as 100 g. They answer different questions.

If your pattern calls for a particular weight, begin there and read any specific yarn recommendations. A weight category alone does not guarantee the right size, drape or texture. For more context, see the Craft Yarn Council's Standard Yarn Weight System.

How much yarn do I need? Calculate skeins from length

Look for a length in metres, yards, or both. The pattern may state a total length for each size, while the yarn label usually gives the length in one package. Keep the units consistent before dividing.

Basic calculation: pattern length needed ÷ length per skein = number of skeins, rounded up to a whole skein.

Imagine a pattern asks for 780 metres and the yarn label says 200 metres per skein:

  1. Divide 780 by 200: the result is 3.9 skeins.
  2. Round up to a whole package: the mathematical minimum is four skeins, or 800 metres.
  3. Consider whether the pattern estimate covers your size and planned changes. Swatching, a longer garment, substitutions and individual working habits can affect the amount used.

As a separate example, if you choose a 10% contingency, multiply 780 m by 1.10 to get 858 m. Divide by 200 m and round up: five skeins. Ten percent is an example, not a universal requirement or a promise that you will have enough. Check the pattern and the retailer's return policy before choosing an allowance.

If the pattern is in yards and the label is in metres, convert one figure first: 1 yard equals 0.9144 metres. Avoid dividing metres directly by yards. For a partial skein, use its remaining length when known; weighing it can support an estimate only if you know the original length and mass and the yarn is comparable throughout.

Fiber content: read beyond the percentage

The fiber line might name a single material or list a blend, such as wool and acrylic. Fiber affects the qualities you may care about: warmth, texture, drape, elasticity and care. Construction matters too, so two yarns with similar percentages are not necessarily interchangeable.

Think about the finished item and its recipient. A frequently washed blanket and a delicate decorative shawl may have different care needs. Check the label and the maker's specific guidance instead of assuming that every yarn with the same fiber name can be washed or worn in the same way. If you are sensitive to a fiber, inspect the full blend and decide what works for you.

Color name and dye lot: check both

The color name or shade number identifies the intended color. A dye lot, when shown, identifies a production batch. Slight differences can appear between lots even when the color name matches, especially across a large area of one-color fabric.

Before buying several skeins, compare their color and lot information. Keep a photo or note of the label in case you need more later. Matching lot numbers reduces one source of variation, but lighting, yarn characteristics and production can still affect appearance. Check the yarn itself in suitable light.

Some yarns are sold as “no dye lot.” That does not mean every skein is guaranteed to look identical. If matching matters, compare available skeins and follow the manufacturer's advice. When you already have different-looking lots, test a small sample or consider alternating skeins where suitable; the result depends on your project.

Label gauge versus pattern gauge

Many labels suggest a hook or needle size and show an expected number of stitches—and sometimes rows—over a stated distance. That label gauge is useful context, but it is not automatically the gauge for your pattern. A pattern may use a different stitch, tool, yarn or fabric density.

For a size-sensitive project, make a swatch using the yarn, tool and stitch pattern specified by your pattern. Prepare and measure it as the pattern directs, including washing or blocking if instructed. Compare both stitch and row gauge when the pattern gives both. Adjust your approach and make another swatch if needed; a different hook or needle size can change the fabric as well as the count.

New to measuring? Our crochet gauge swatch guide explains the process. For crochet tools, compare the actual millimetre measurement as described in the hook-size guide.

Yarn label symbols and care instructions

Yarn care symbols are shorthand for the maker's instructions. They may cover washing, drying, ironing, bleaching or professional cleaning. Read the whole label: a symbol can include a temperature, dots or an additional mark that changes its meaning. When in doubt, check the manufacturer's explanation or the Craft Yarn Council's care-symbol guide.

Keep care information with the finished item or pass it to the recipient. When a project combines yarns or other materials with different requirements, consider every component and follow the most restrictive applicable instructions. A label's care guidance is not a substitute for checking how the finished item is constructed.

Comparing a substitute yarn

A yarn in the same weight category is a useful candidate, not an automatic replacement. Before buying, compare:

  1. Total length: Calculate the skeins using the substitute's length per package, not the original yarn's package count.
  2. Fiber and construction: Consider care, stretch, warmth, texture and drape for the intended project.
  3. Gauge and fabric: Swatch in the pattern's stitch and evaluate the fabric, not just the number of stitches.
  4. Color and dye lot: Check that the required quantity is available in a suitable match.
  5. Care: Make sure the finished item can be maintained as intended.

For example, replacing a 200 m skein with a 150 m skein changes the number of packages you need even if the skeins have the same labeled weight category. A 780 m project would need at least six 150 m skeins mathematically: 780 ÷ 150 = 5.2, rounded up. Recheck the pattern and any allowance separately.

What to record before you lose the label

Keep the yarn name and maker, fiber content, weight category, length and mass per package, color, dye lot, quantity and care instructions. Add the project name and any notes about your swatch. A photo of both sides of the band can help, but make sure important numbers are readable.

A paper notebook or a phone note works. If you prefer keeping project details together, StitchCount includes a yarn stash for recording details such as colors, dye lots and quantities alongside your knitting and crochet projects. Use whichever method you will be able to find again.

Quick answers

Does a 100 g skein always have the same yardage?

No. One hundred grams describes mass; the length depends on the yarn. Read the metres or yards printed on each label when calculating quantity.

Can I use the recommended hook or needle size without making a swatch?

The label's size is a starting point. Whether you need a swatch depends on the project, but it is particularly useful where fit or finished dimensions matter. Compare your result with the pattern's instructions.

What if the label has no dye-lot number?

Check the manufacturer's product information and compare the skeins themselves. A missing lot number does not prove the shades are identical.
