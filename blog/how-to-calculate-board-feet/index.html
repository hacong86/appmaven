How to Calculate Board Feet: Formula, Units and Lumber Cost

Calculate board feet with inches or feet, work through a 4 BF example, and separate lumber volume from the supplier’s pricing tally.

AppMaven TeamOctober 18, 2026Woodworking · Practical guide

In this guide

  1. The formula and its units
  2. Worked examples
  3. From board feet to cost
  4. Before you order

A board can be eight feet long without containing eight board feet. Length tells you how far the board extends; board feet describe its volume. That distinction matters when you compare two boards with different widths or thicknesses.

The formula and its units

One board foot equals 144 cubic inches: the volume of a piece 1 inch thick, 12 inches wide and 12 inches long. Penn State Extension gives the following formula for sawn lumber. [1]

BF = thickness (in) × width (in) × length (ft) ÷ 12

Keep thickness and width in inches and length in feet for this version. If every measurement is in inches, use the other denominator:

BF = thickness (in) × width (in) × length (in) ÷ 144

The two formulas agree because one foot contains 12 inches. Changing the unit without changing the denominator is an easy way to make an answer twelve times too large or too small.

Worked examples

One board: 1 inch × 6 inches × 8 feet

Write the unit next to each measurement before entering the numbers. The calculation is 1 × 6 × 8 ÷ 12 = 4 BF. As a cross-check, convert 8 feet to 96 inches: 1 × 6 × 96 ÷ 144 also equals 4 BF.

For the last row, one board contains 1.5 × 8 × 6 ÷ 12 = 6 BF. Two identical boards contain 12 BF. When board sizes differ, calculate each row separately and add the results; multiplying one average-looking board by the total quantity can hide a size difference.

From board feet to cost

For a hypothetical quote of $7.50 per BF, a 12 BF tally costs 12 × $7.50 = $90 before any separate tax, delivery or processing charges. This is an arithmetic example, not a current lumber price.

Confirm which dimensions and rounding rules the seller uses for the invoice. Wood-Mizer’s board-foot guide illustrates lumber tallies; a seller’s tally basis and the physical volume of a surfaced board need not use the same inputs. [2] A small measured-volume result is not, by itself, proof that the bill is wrong.

Before you order

  1. Label thickness, width, length and quantity explicitly.
  2. Keep each row’s units consistent.
  3. Confirm the supplier’s tally and price basis before comparing quotes.
  4. List extra stock for your project separately from the calculated volume. A purchase allowance is a planning choice, not part of the definition of a board foot.

Working from a nominal size such as 2×4? Read nominal versus actual lumber sizes before deciding which dimensions belong in your calculation.
