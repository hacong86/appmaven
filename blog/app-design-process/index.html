App Design Process for Solo Android Developers: 7 Steps

AppMaven Studio
Independent Android developer
Jan 5, 2026
8 min read

Most design advice assumes a team with a researcher, a designer and a developer. If you build Android apps alone or with one or two people, you play all of those roles, and time is your scarcest resource. This guide turns the usual design process into seven practical steps that fit a small team, with links to the official Android and Google Play documentation behind each one.

AppMaven Studio is an independent developer that publishes Android apps and games on Google Play. What follows is a general guide for Android developers, not a description of a large studio workflow.

Step 1: Write Down One Clear Job for the App

Before you sketch anything, write one sentence that says who the app is for, what they are trying to get done and what gets in their way. A simple template is "[person] wants to [job], but [obstacle]". For example: "A home cook wants to scale a recipe to a different number of servings, but doing the math by hand is slow and error-prone." Keep that sentence visible while you design. When you are unsure whether a screen or feature belongs in the app, check it against the sentence.

  • Name the one task the first version must do well.
  • List what the app will not do in version one. This list protects your schedule.
  • Describe how people solve the problem today: paper, a spreadsheet, a chat with themselves, or another app.
  • Decide what success looks like in plain terms, for example "a first-time user completes the main task without help".

Step 2: Study Competitors on Google Play

Google Play is free research material. Search for the words your users would type, open the top results and treat them as a reference library rather than a template.

  • Read the store listings. Note what each title and short description promises and which screenshot appears first. That tells you which benefit the developer thinks matters most.
  • Read the reviews, especially the middle ratings. Two- and three-star reviews often explain what almost works. Sort by most recent so you see current problems, not ones fixed years ago.
  • Collect recurring complaints. Intrusive ads, confusing setup, missing export or a stretched tablet layout are design requirements for your app.
  • Install two or three apps and do the main task in each one. Count the taps and note where you hesitate.

Do not copy layouts, text or graphics. You are looking for unmet needs and for conventions that users already expect, such as where settings live or how a list is sorted.

Step 3: Scope a Small First Version

Small teams rarely fail because the first version was too small. Pick the smallest version that completes the job from step 1 from start to finish, then write down the list of screens it needs.

  • One core flow that works end to end, including saving the result.
  • Only the settings that the core flow needs.
  • Accounts, sync, themes and sharing deferred unless the job depends on them.
  • For every screen: what the user sees on first launch, while loading, when there is no data and when something fails.

Empty, loading and error states are part of the design, not polish to add later. A first-time user always meets the empty state first, so it should explain what to do next.

Step 4: Sketch on Paper, Then Build Low-Fidelity Wireframes

Paper sketches cost minutes and are easy to throw away, which is exactly what you want at this stage. Draw the core flow as a strip of phone-shaped boxes, one per screen, and walk through it as if you were a new user. Then turn the strongest version into greyscale wireframes in any tool you like.

  • One primary action per screen. If two buttons compete for attention, one of them probably belongs somewhere else.
  • Order information by importance, not by how it is stored in your database.
  • Use realistic content: long names, large numbers and translated strings break layouts that looked fine with short placeholder text.
  • Leave color and branding for later. Greyscale keeps feedback focused on flow and hierarchy.

If you build with Jetpack Compose, previews make cheap wireframes too. The Android adaptive layout guide points to preview annotations such as @PreviewScreenSizes, @PreviewFontScale and @PreviewLightDark, which show the same composable at several screen sizes, font scales and in light and dark themes.

Step 5: Follow Material Design 3 and Plan for Every Screen Size

Material Design 3 is Google's design system for Android. Following it means your controls behave the way Android users expect, and you get tested components instead of designing every button yourself. In Compose, the Material 3 library builds a theme from three parts: a color scheme, typography and shapes. On Android 12 and higher it can also use dynamic color derived from the user's wallpaper, with your own light and dark schemes as the fallback.

Use Window Size Classes, Not Device Checks

Your app will run on phones, foldables and tablets, often in split-screen. Android's window size classes group the space available to your app into a few breakpoints. Width is usually the one that matters:

  • Compact: narrower than 600dp, which covers almost all phones in portrait.
  • Medium: 600dp to under 840dp, such as most tablets and large unfolded displays in portrait.
  • Expanded: 840dp to under 1200dp, such as most tablets in landscape.
  • Large and extra-large: 1200dp and wider, for large tablets and desktop displays.

The class describes the app window, not the device, and it can change while the app runs when the user rotates, folds or resizes it. Android's documentation says explicitly that size classes are not meant for "is this a tablet" logic. In Compose you read the current class with currentWindowAdaptiveInfo().windowSizeClass.

  • Navigation: a bottom navigation bar on compact windows and a navigation rail on larger ones. NavigationSuiteScaffold switches between them for you.
  • Lists and details: show the list and the selected item side by side on expanded windows and one at a time on smaller ones. ListDetailPaneScaffold implements this pattern.
  • Keep state when the window changes, so a rotation or fold does not reset what the user was doing.

This also matters for discovery. The adaptive apps overview states that Google Play ranks apps optimized for large screens higher than unoptimized ones, and that apps which do not meet its large screen quality standards can show a warning on their details page.

Step 6: Design for Accessibility From the First Sketch

Accessibility is much cheaper to design in than to retrofit. Android's accessibility guidelines give concrete targets you can check in a wireframe:

  • Touch targets of at least 48dp by 48dp for every interactive element. Material components such as Button and IconButton already enforce this; custom controls need it set by hand.
  • Text contrast of at least 4.5:1 for text smaller than 18sp, or bold text smaller than 14sp, and at least 3:1 for other text.
  • A description for every meaningful icon or image that states its purpose ("Share", not "Share button"). Purely decorative images get no description so screen readers skip them.
  • Cues other than color. Pair color with text, icons or shape so people with color vision deficiencies can tell options apart.
  • Room for larger text. Users can raise the system font size. Size text in sp and check that labels wrap instead of being cut off.

Then test on a real device with TalkBack, the Android screen reader that announces content aloud, and check color contrast with an online contrast checker or the Accessibility Scanner app, as Android's guidelines suggest.

Step 7: Test With a Few People, Then With Google Play Testing Tracks

Quick Usability Tests

You do not need a lab. In his article "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users", Jakob Nielsen argues that a test with five users finds about 85% of the usability problems, and that three studies of five users each, with fixes in between, improve a design more than one large study. The five-user figure applies to comparable users. If your app serves very different groups, the article suggests three to four users from each group when there are two groups, and three per group when there are more.

  • Give each person a goal ("add a recipe and double it"), not step-by-step instructions.
  • Ask them to think aloud, and do not help unless they are completely stuck.
  • Write down where they hesitate, tap the wrong thing or ask a question.
  • Fix the biggest problems, then test again with new people.

Google Play Testing Tracks

Once you have a build, Play Console offers three ways to run an internal, closed or open test:

  • Internal testing: up to 100 testers per app, and a new build becomes available to them within minutes. Useful for checking the real install and update path.
  • Closed testing: testers you invite through email lists (up to 2,000 people per list) or Google Groups.
  • Open testing: anyone can join and send you private feedback. You can leave it unlimited or set a cap of at least 1,000 testers.

Feedback from testers does not affect your public rating. If you have a personal developer account created after November 13, 2023, Google Play requires a closed test with at least 12 testers who stay opted in for at least 14 consecutive days before you can apply for production access, so plan that time into your schedule.

After Launch: Keep Designing With Real Data

Launch is where you finally see how the design holds up across many devices and people. Play Console gives a small team three useful feedback loops:

  • Android vitals: tracks stability, including the user-perceived crash rate and ANR (app not responding) rate. The bad behavior thresholds are 1.09% of daily users for crashes and 0.47% of daily active users for ANRs overall, and 8% on any single device model. Apps above them are likely to be less discoverable on Google Play.
  • Ratings and reviews: you can post one public reply per review, and the reviewer is notified. Filter by app version and device to connect complaints to a specific release or screen size.
  • Store listing experiments: once the app is published, you can A/B test the icon, feature graphic, screenshots and descriptions with up to two variants against the current listing.

Treat each update as another small round of the same process: pick one problem from vitals, reviews or your own tests, design a fix, test it with a few people and ship it.

Key Takeaways

  • Start from one written job the app does, and list what version one will not do.
  • Use Google Play listings and reviews to find unmet needs, not layouts to copy.
  • Design empty, loading and error states as part of the first version.
  • Follow Material Design 3 and make layout decisions with window size classes, not device checks.
  • Build in 48dp touch targets, sufficient contrast, labels and larger text from the first sketch.
  • Run small usability tests with about five people, fix, and test again.
  • Use internal, closed and open testing on Google Play, then keep improving with vitals and reviews.
