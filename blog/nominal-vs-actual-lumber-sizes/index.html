Why a 2×4 Is Usually 1½ × 3½ Inches

Understand common U.S. nominal lumber labels, check typical actual dimensions, and avoid a one-inch layout error before building.

AppMaven TeamOctober 18, 2026Woodworking · Practical guide

In this guide

  1. What the size label means
  2. Common examples
  3. A layout error you can check
  4. What to measure before building

You buy a board labelled 2×4, put a tape across its end, and find about 1½ inches by 3½ inches. For typical U.S. dry, surfaced softwood lumber, that is an expected difference between the size name and the actual cross-section.

What the size label means

Nominal size is the common name used to identify the lumber. Actual size is the dimension of the processed piece. Drying and surfacing affect the resulting dimensions; Lowe’s explains this distinction and gives examples of nominal and actual sizes. [1]

Use the nominal label when you are identifying what product to buy. Use dimensions appropriate to your actual stock when you are drawing a joint, checking a fit or calculating a clear opening. Keeping two clearly named fields—“product label” and “measured size”—is more useful than trying to make one number serve both jobs.

Common examples

Examples from Lowe’s lumber guide; verify the product and the board in hand
Nominal labelTypical actual size
1 × 4 × 8 ft¾ in × 3½ in × 8 ft
2 × 4 × 10 ft1½ in × 3½ in × 10 ft
4 × 4 × 8 ft3½ in × 3½ in × 8 ft

These examples describe common U.S. lumber sizes. They are not a conversion rule for every country, rough-sawn board, green timber, hardwood product or sheet material. Check the product specification instead of applying a blanket “subtract half an inch” rule.

A layout error you can check

Imagine a simple, nonstructural layout with a target outside width of 24 inches. Two side pieces occupy that width, and each side measures 1½ inches thick. With a plain opening between their inside faces, the remaining width is:

24 − 1½ − 1½ = 21 inches

If you had used the nominal 2-inch label for each side, you would have written 24 − 2 − 2 = 20 inches. That is a full inch of difference. This example only checks the clear opening; it does not include a rabbet, dado, tenon or another joint that changes a part’s required length.

Write the assembly’s outside dimension, then subtract the pieces that actually occupy it. If a joint adds material inside the side pieces, show that as a separate step. This makes the source of each number visible when you review the drawing later.

What to measure before building

  • Thickness and width: record the stock you intend to use, with units.
  • Usable length: confirm the piece is long enough for the parts and the cutting allowances you have planned.
  • Critical fits: check the opening or adjoining part that determines the final size.
  • Material identity: keep the product label alongside the measured dimensions so the shopping list still makes sense.

For a parts list, keep these measured inputs separate from a nominal shopping label. Our cut-list guide shows one way to record finished dimensions and fitting notes together.
